Quality reviewed

This is a benchmark paper introducing Sci-MMR, a dataset of 235 multi-hop evidence-grounded scientific reasoning tasks built from peer-reviewed publications. The paper demonstrates strong contribution clarity through a formal argument graph representation, methodological rigor through a human-in-the-loop construction pipeline with expert verification, and evaluation strength through four diagnostic settings and three complementary metrics. The central claims about the gap between answer accuracy and evidence coverage are directly supported by quantitative results across eight frontier models. The error attribution analysis provides fine-grained evidence for the two identified bottlenecks. Related work is adequately positioned through structured comparison with existing benchmarks. Limitations are explicitly acknowledged, including subjectivity in graph construction and LLM evaluator bias. The paper includes extensive appendices documenting the construction pipeline, evaluation settings, prompt catalogue, and human validation, supporting reproducibility. The main scientific uncertainty is that the benchmark's argument graphs represent one plausible reconstruction rather than unique ground truth, and the evaluation relies on LLM-based judging, though human agreement is reported. Overall, the paper provides a well-validated benchmark with clear scientific value for evaluating evidence-grounded reasoning in multimodal agents.

The primary scientific uncertainty is the subjectivity inherent in argument graph construction: the paper acknowledges that graphs represent one plausible reconstruction of a paper's evidential structure rather than unique ground truth. While expert verification mitigates this, alternative valid interpretations of evidence granularity and support relations may exist. Additionally, the evaluation metrics assess evidence and claims explicitly expressed in final responses rather than internal reasoning processes, which may not fully capture a model's evidence-grounded reasoning. The LLM-based evaluation, despite reported human agreement, may still be affected by semantic ambiguity and evaluator bias. The benchmark's temporal distribution is heavily weighted toward 2026 papers (54.5%), which may limit generalizability to older scientific literature. These limitations are acknowledged but not
