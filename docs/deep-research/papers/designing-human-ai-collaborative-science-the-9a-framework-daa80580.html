    Scientific inquiry develops through contributions that are proposed, tested, questioned, revised, and sometimes abandoned. Research on the social epistemology of science emphasizes critical inter- action and responsiveness to criticism as conditions for collective inquiry [ 17]. These contributions may introduce new hypotheses, evidence, methods, or objections that require further examination. For collaborative scientific work to progress, participants need to determine how such contributions are handled and preserve the reasoning behind consequential decisions. We useaccountabilityto describe this process: scientific contributions and the responses to them remain traceable, including what was examined, what concerns were raised, and why particular conclusions or actions were retained, revised, or rejected.     Canonical source evidence
    The framework is conceptual, and its proposed relationships have yet to be tested in real scientific settings. Scientific domains differ in research duration, task risks, available evidence, and dependence on expert judgment, so appropriate activity granularity, authorization thresholds, and coordination intensity may vary across contexts. Explicit information sharing, review, and record-keeping also impose computational and human costs. For bounded, low-risk, and easily verifiable tasks, simple AI assistance or predominantly autonomous execution may be more appropriate than elaborate collaborative arrangements. More broadly, widespread reliance on similar AI models across research teams may also raise concerns about convergence in scientific questions, methods, and research directions. Such collective effects raise broader questions of epistemic responsibility [20].
