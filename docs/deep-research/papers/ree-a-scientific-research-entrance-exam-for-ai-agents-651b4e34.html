Abstract / summary

Autonomous research agents are emerging as a promising approach to scientific problem-solving across disciplines, owing to their growing ability to improve a solution through repeated experiments. Quality reviewed

The study offers a rigorous, well-controlled benchmark design solving known leakage and fairness issues in agent evaluation through held-out test instances and identical environment re-runs of human expert baselines, backed by clear reporting and transparent methodology.

The authors explicitly delineate core limitations: evaluation is limited to tasks solvable and verifiable purely computationally within offline software containers, results represent single long-horizon runs per agent-task pair without intra-task repeat variance, and attribution analysis relies in part on an LLM judge.

    REE's problems are research problems that scientific communities pose, evaluate, and continue to study. From such problems, we selected those that meet three criteria. The first is that the problem is open-ended, which means that a solution is a program scored by a quantitative metric over many instances, and it is never simply marked right or wrong. The second is that it can be evaluated automatically on instances that the agent has not seen, and the third is that it has an established human-designed solution that can be run within the budget of the task. Each selected problem keeps the objective and the evaluation metric that its field uses, so a solution is judged by the field's own measure. In building gene trees from protein sequences, for example, a solution is scored by the number of branchings that differ from a reference tree, which a generic distance loss does not capture.

    REE covers research problems whose solutions a program can score on held-out instances within a fixed time limit, so research that needs physical experiments, new data collection, or human judgment of a result lies outside its scope. Each score comes from a single run, and the bootstrap intervals resample tasks, so they describe the spread of scores over tasks and do not describe the variation between repeated runs on one task. Each agent also runs under one budget and in one harness, with 12 hours of wall-clock time at its highest reasoning effort, and other budgets or harnesses may give other scores. For most tasks the upper bound is the best value that the metric can take by definition, so the distance to it measures room on the scale, which a method may not be able to close.
