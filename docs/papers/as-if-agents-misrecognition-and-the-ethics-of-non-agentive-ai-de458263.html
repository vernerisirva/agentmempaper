Quality reviewed

This is a clearly structured philosophical position paper. The central claim—that current AI lacks episodic memory and therefore cannot be a responsible epistemic testifier, making widespread trust a form of misrecognition—is stated precisely and repeatedly. The argument is developed through distinct sections: establishing the practical phenomenon of AI epistemic authority, defining episodic vs. semantic memory, arguing current AI lacks episodic memory, deriving implications for epistemic authority, and sketching an ethics of non-agentive AI. The paper engages substantively with relevant philosophical and empirical literature, including Hauswald, Shin, Fricker, Tulving, and recent AI memory research. It explicitly addresses a key objection (that episodic memory may not be necessary for epistemic agency) and narrows its scope to current systems. Limitations are handled through careful scope restrictions and acknowledgment of background issues such as experience. The main weakness is that the empirical evidence for the phenomenon of AI authority is cited rather than directly analyzed, and the argument relies on conceptual distinctions that are not empirically tested, but this is appropriate for the paper type.

The paper's central empirical premise—that users treat AI as epistemic authorities—is supported by citations to studies (Logg et al., Tun et al., Shekar et al., etc.) but the supplied text does not include detailed methodological assessment of those studies. The philosophical argument depends on the claim that episodic memory is necessary for robust epistemic agency, which is defended conceptually but remains contestable. The paper acknowledges this by narrowing its claim to current systems and explicitly addressing the objection that episodic memory may not be the only route to agency. The absence of a formal limitations section is partially mitigated by in-text scope restrictions and acknowledgment of the deeper issue of experience.
