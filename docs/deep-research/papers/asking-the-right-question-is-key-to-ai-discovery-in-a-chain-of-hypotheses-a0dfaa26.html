    Artificial intelligence (AI) scientists pursue discovery through a chain of hypotheses, fitting candidate models to existing observations and often choosing experiments where those models disagree most. Yet when early data miss a narrow feature, model selection favors simple curves that agree across the unmeasured gap, so disagreement-guided search has no reason to look where the new phenomenon lies. When we tested two frontier AI scientists, Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1-Sol, across 32 closed-loop trials with a narrow peak omittedfromthestartingdata,bothsampledinsidethepeakregionlessoftenthanrandomsearch. Surprisingly, the agent that followed model disagreement more closely missed the peak in all 32 trials, whereas discovery succeeded when experiments probed the unmeasured gap early and followed up across the anomaly. Simple models fitted to the observed background then agree across the unmeasured gap and differ only over minor details elsewhere. Following their disagreement steers experiments away from that gap rather than into it, much as confirmation bias keeps human researchers testing what their working theories already anticipate25–27. To test how experimental choices shape autonomous discovery, we evaluated two frontier AI scientists, Claude Sonnet 5.5 and GPT-6.1-Sol, in closed-loop equation-discovery trials where a narrow peak lay inside an initially unmeasured region. Both agents probed that region less often than random search, and the agent that followed equation disagreement more closely missed the peak in every trial as it spent its budget separating nearly identical background curves.
